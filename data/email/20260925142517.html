Groq — in its own words (qwen/qwen3.8-27b)

On listing (25). The subscription data shows the IPO is significantly undersubscribed (approx. 0.99x) as of the last recorded timestamp, indicating weak initial demand from investors. This lack of traction suggests the market is skeptical of the valuation or the business fundamentals, making a listing pop unlikely without a significant price cut or late-stage institutional intervention.

On the long term (40). While revenue and EBITDA margins are improving, the profit after tax (PAT) margin is extremely thin (3.07%) and volatile, with a massive spike in the latest year that may not be sustainable. The high debt-to-equity ratio (1.17) and significant litigation risks create a fragile foundation for long-term value creation, especially given the low cash conversion of profits.

On governance (35). The prospectus reveals a pattern of regulatory non-compliance, including a show cause notice for failing to establish a Vigil Mechanism for multiple years and various tax and GST disputes. These issues suggest weak internal controls and a history of friction with regulators, which is a red flag for minority shareholders.

NVIDIA — in its own words (moonshotai/kimi-k3)

On listing (38). Demand is weak: the book is only 0.99x subscribed with the close still days away, which for a commodity steel IPO with 0.2% PAT margin one year ago is a lukewarm signal at best. Pricing at ~21.9x latest basic EPS is below the 45.2x benchmark, so a modest pop is possible if the book fills, but there is no grey-market or anchor data to support conviction either way.

On the long term (32). This is an undifferentiated steel manufacturer with 4.9-7.3% EBITDA margins, pathetically thin PAT margins (0.22-3.07%), leverage of 1.17x debt/equity, and operating cash flow that converts only ~21% of EBITDA in the latest period — a perennial working-capital-hungry cyclical. The PAT CAGR of 81% is a base-effect artefact off a near-zero profit year, not earnings power, and revenue has compounded only ~4% over two years in a volume business. ROCE of 12.9% is improving but remains barely above the likely cost of capital.

On governance (40). The pattern is uncomfortable: unpaid-fee guarantees for promoter companies, a credit facility for a related party in which directors are interested, a ₹5,374 lakh bank guarantee given for a party-related loan already provided for, a SEBI penalty under fraudulent/unfair-trade-practice regulations, an ROC notice for non-disclosure of the board-mandated vigil mechanism for three consecutive years, and income-tax reassessment orders [promoters, page 560][related_party, page 607][litigation, page 627][litigation, page 637]. No single item is disqualifying, together they describe a promoter-controlled company slow to separate itself from its directors' interests.

Key risks it identified

  • Working capital consumes earnings — Only ~21% of EBITDA turned into operating cash in the latest year while receivable days stretched from ~46 to 58.5, meaning growth is funded by debt or unpaid receivables, not profit. [business, page 333]
  • Leverage and lender dependence — Debt exceeds equity (1.17x) and the company was still drawing emergency credit guarantee loans and giving related-party guarantees after the balance-sheet date, leaving thin margins with no buffer against a steel-price downturn. [promoters, page 560]
  • Related-party entanglement — Guarantees for promoter group companies and credit facilities for a related party in which directors are interested mean shareholder cash flow routes around the listed entity's interests. [related_party, page 607]
  • Regulatory history — A paid SEBI penalty under fraudulent-trade-practice rules, an ROC vigil-mechanism lapse across three years, and a ₹458 lakh income-tax reassessment raise the cost of surprises in a seller-friendly prospectus. [litigation, page 627]
  • Trading-revenue mix — Revenue includes traded raw materials and intermediate products (scrap, iron ore, billet), which inflates sales growth and depresses margins further — the 17% latest revenue growth may overstate manufacturing momentum. [business, page 339]
